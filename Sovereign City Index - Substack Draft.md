# The Sovereign City Index

**Ranking the world's planned cities by the gap between what was announced and what is actually on the ground.**

*The most expensive lie in twenty-first-century urbanism is the rendering. Saudi Arabia spent the last decade selling a 170-kilometre linear city; in 2024 the actual buildable phase was conceded to be roughly 2.4. Country Garden announced a 700,000-resident Forest City off Johor; the resident count is in the low five figures and the developer has defaulted. Egypt opened a New Administrative Capital with offices for thirty-four ministries; the visible occupancy is a few hundred employees rotating through what was sold as a six-million-person metropolis. Each of these projects raised real money, broke real ground, and produced a real photograph. None of them has produced the city in the rendering. The Sovereign City Index is an attempt to score that gap explicitly.*

## What the index measures

A "realism composite" of four dimensions, each scored 0 to 25, summing to 0 to 100. The dimensions are deliberately chosen to be observable from public reporting rather than from announcement materials, because announcement materials are the thing being measured against.

**Capital deployment ratio.** Announced spend versus disclosed actual spend, where actual spend is anchored to public-private filings, sovereign budget line items, contractor pay schedules, and credible third-party reporting. Vaporware concentrates here. A project that announces 500 billion and demonstrably spends 80 scores low on this dimension regardless of what is built; a project that announces 8 and spends 8 scores high.

**Population yield.** Verified resident headcount divided by the stated target population at the present year. Ghost cities concentrate here. The denominator is whatever the project's own published timeline says it should hit by now; the numerator is residents who actually sleep there. Construction workers and rotating government employees do not count.

**Governance autonomy.** Whether the city has the legal, regulatory, and fiscal authority to do what its plan requires. A free zone with its own visa and tax framework scores higher than a project sitting under conventional municipal control. A project under direct sovereign or military authority scores lower than one with a chartered authority and a publicly accountable governance board, because direct sovereign authority correlates with downside protection rather than upside execution.

**Milestone fidelity.** The fraction of publicly announced milestones met on the originally stated timeline, with explicit credit for projects that revised their targets honestly before the market revised them involuntarily. The Line lost almost all its milestone-fidelity score in 2024 not because it cannot build a 2.4-kilometre segment, but because the 170-kilometre version was the publicly stated milestone and the revision was forced by reality rather than chosen as a planning decision.

The composite is the sum. Brasília, scored as a 1960 calibration case rather than a present-day index entry, sits at roughly 85; the canonical successful sovereign city. Putrajaya at 1995 launch sits in the high 60s. The Line in 2026 sits in the low teens. The point of the dimension construction is that the same city can carry a strong score on capital deployment and a poor score on population yield, and that pattern is itself the story.

A note on what this ranking is and is not. It is not a verdict on whether the project will eventually succeed; Songdo at twenty years out has had ample opportunity to fall further than it has. It is not a verdict on whether the underlying vision was worth pursuing; Nusantara is among the most geopolitically rational capital-relocation projects on this list and still scores in the bottom four because the realism dimensions do not yet have material under them. The score is a snapshot of the announcement-to-reality gap as it stands in 2026.

## Six exemplars

### [Astana / Nur-Sultan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astana)

The textbook case at the top of the index. Kazakhstan's capital relocation from Almaty was announced in 1994, designated in 1997, and operational by 1998. The headline numbers are unusual for a planned-city project: target population of one million by 2020, actual population approximately 1.4 million; announced capital spend of roughly 30 billion dollars, demonstrably deployed; Expo 2017 hosted on the announced site, on the announced timeline. The scoring weakness is governance autonomy; the city is a sovereign capital under a centralised regime, which earns the structural-functioning bonus on capital deployment but caps the autonomy dimension. The lesson Astana teaches is that the most reliable way to build a planned city is to relocate a functioning state apparatus into it on a forced timeline. The capital-relocation cases on this list outperform the speculative-development cases by a wide and consistent margin.

### [Putrajaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putrajaya)

The mid-table calibration case. Malaysia's federal administrative capital was announced in 1995, broke ground in 1996, and absorbed the federal government's relocation between 1999 and 2003. Where Putrajaya loses points is on population yield. The city was sold as a 320,000-resident administrative metropolis by 2010; the actual count in 2024 sits closer to 110,000. Where it gains points is on milestone fidelity, capital deployment honesty, and governance: the Federal Territory designation gave Putrajaya the autonomy it needed to function as a working capital from day one, and the spending profile tracked the plan. Putrajaya is what a sovereign city looks like when the government does not let its own marketing inflate the resident-count target into something the underlying labor market cannot absorb. Most cities on this list would benefit from the Putrajaya discount of dividing announcement-day population claims by three before scoring credibility.

### [Songdo International Business District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songdo_International_Business_District)

The mid-table partial-success case. Songdo was announced in 2003 as a smart-city tabula rasa anchoring a Free Economic Zone inside Incheon, with a target of 350,000 residents and a multinational professional class that would treat Northeast Asia's logistical centroid as a base of operations. Twenty-three years on, the resident count is roughly 150,000 and the multinational professional class largely did not arrive. What Songdo demonstrates is the dimension that does not show up in the four-axis scoring: a planned city's anchor strategy has to survive contact with the macroeconomic environment. The Free Economic Zone framework was the right autonomy posture; the smart-city infrastructure works; the Cisco partnership was a credible anchor in 2003. None of that fully compensated for the gravitational pull of Seoul thirty kilometres away. Songdo is the case the next ten projects on this list should study.

### [New Administrative Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Administrative_Capital) (Egypt)

The high-spend, low-yield case. Announced in 2015, partly opened in 2023, the NAC is intended to relocate the Egyptian government and absorb six and a half million long-term residents into the desert east of Cairo. The capital deployment ratio is the strongest dimension of the index for the project: roughly 58 billion dollars announced, with demonstrable deployment in infrastructure, government office construction, the central business district towers, and the Iconic Tower (Africa's tallest). The weakness is governance autonomy. The Administrative Capital for Urban Development (ACUD) holding company is majority-owned by the Egyptian military, which produces a project that can deploy capital at scale but cannot easily attract the private-sector commercial occupancy the city's economic case depends on. Population yield in the present year is well under five percent of the long-term target, and the relocated workforce is mostly rotating government rather than resident. The NAC is the index's clearest illustration that sovereign autonomy and project autonomy point in opposite directions, and that a project run by the parent state's most powerful unitary actor is harder to populate, not easier.

### [Nusantara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nusantara_(city))

The early-phase case. Indonesia's planned new capital on East Kalimantan was announced in 2019, legislated in 2022, and ceremonially inaugurated for Independence Day 2024. The Otorita Ibu Kota Nusantara (OIKN) is a credibly-structured authority with proper enabling legislation and a governance design closer to Putrajaya's than to the NAC's. The realism gap is everywhere else. Capital deployment is running below the announced 33 billion dollar profile and the foreign investment that the original plan assumed never materialised. The Prabowo administration in late 2024 quietly began revising the timeline, which under this index's scoring rule preserves milestone fidelity points (an honest revision before market forces it). Resident population at the present year is in the hundreds rather than the millions targeted by 2045. Nusantara could plausibly climb this index over the next ten years; it could equally plausibly become this list's third-largest cautionary tale. The honest read is that it has not yet generated enough material to score high on any dimension other than governance design.

### [The Line / NEOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neom)

The bottom-of-index canonical case. NEOM was announced in 2017 as a 500-billion-dollar special economic zone in northwest Saudi Arabia, with The Line announced in 2021 as a 170-kilometre, 9-million-resident linear city to be substantially complete by 2030. The 2024 admission that the buildable phase one was being scaled to roughly 2.4 kilometres collapsed the project's milestone fidelity score by an order of magnitude. The other dimensions reflect the same reality. Capital deployment is running well below the announced profile despite headline-level investment. Resident population is a few dozen executives and somewhat larger numbers of construction workers in temporary accommodation. Sindalah, the luxury island component, did open in 2024 on a credible timeline and a credible population scale, which is the only place the project gains points on this index. Trojena, the ski resort hosting the 2029 Asian Winter Games, has a chance to gain similar credit if it delivers on time. The structural problem with The Line is that the Crown Prince's direct authority is the dimension protecting the project from cancellation and the dimension preventing it from being honestly re-scoped. Authority and accountability are not a substitute for each other in planned-city construction.

## What the index actually predicts

Three things, none of them the things planned-city marketing materials usually emphasise.

First, capital-relocation projects systematically outperform speculative-development projects on this index, and the gap is large enough to be the index's headline finding. Astana, Putrajaya, and Naypyidaw all relocated functioning state apparatus into a planned site on a forced timeline, and all three sit in the top half. Songdo, Konza, Eko Atlantic, Forest City, and The Line are speculative-development plays where the city has to attract its own population on its own merits, and all five sit in the bottom half. The single most reliable predictor of a planned city succeeding is whether it has a sovereign function being relocated into it. The market does not on its own fill an empty city.

Second, the governance autonomy dimension matters less for whether a project gets built and more for whether it can be honestly re-scoped when the original plan does not survive contact with reality. Putrajaya could quietly accept that 110,000 was the actual sustainable population and move on; The Line could not concede the 170-kilometre target until external pressure forced the admission. The cities with chartered authorities and accountable governance boards close the announcement-to-reality gap incrementally over time. The cities under direct sovereign authority either deliver the original plan or carry the reputational debt of the deferred admission. Both outcomes are observable on this list.

Third, the index suggests a category of urbanism that the conventional "city rankings" debate systematically under-discusses: the planned city as a deliberately scaled-down version of its own announcement. The most successful cases on this list are the ones whose ten-year residents represent thirty to fifty percent of the original target. The least successful are the ones still claiming the original target. Modesty is structural protection in this category. The next round of planned-city announcements would benefit from announcing a target at the realism-discounted level rather than at the marketing-grade aspiration level.

The Sovereign City Index is the [Academic Gravity Wells](https://rankings.citizenofnowhere.org/badges/academic-gravity-wells) move applied to a different category: take a class of cities the conventional rankings under-frame, build a single-purpose lens that surfaces what they actually have in common, and rank them on the dimension that the announcement copy obscures. The realism gap is the dimension. The index will refresh annually, and the cohort will expand as new projects clear the inclusion thresholds.
