Reading the Oxford Economics Global Cities Index Against Our Own

Where the rigorous read agrees with us, and where it doesn't.

Oxford Economics published the 2025 edition of its Global Cities Index in May. It is one of the most carefully built city rankings on the market. It also makes three editorial choices I think are wrong, and the choices matter.

A serious peer index

If you build a city ranking, you eventually have to say something honest about the other rankings. The list is shorter than people assume. Most "best cities" lists in the press are travel-magazine hot takes with a coat of methodology. The serious ones, the indices that academics and corporate strategy teams actually cite, fit on one hand. Globalization and World Cities (GaWC) at Loughborough is the longest running. Mori Memorial Foundation's Global Power City Index is the most balanced. EIU's Liveability and Mercer's Quality of Living Survey are the gold standard for relocation. Z/Yen's Global Financial Centres Index owns the finance-hub question.

Oxford Economics' Global Cities Index belongs in that group. Its 2025 edition came out in May. It scores 1,000 cities across 27 indicators in five categories, weighted 30/25/25/10/10 across Economics, Human Capital, Quality of Life, Environment, and Governance. The data sits on top of Oxford Economics' Global Cities Forecasting Service, a paid product covering 1,000 metro economies with multi-decade projections, which is why their indicators are forward-looking in a way most static rankings are not. They use OECD Functional Urban Area definitions, which is the cleanest cross-country urban geography available in the public-sector toolkit.

This year they introduced something genuinely new: an eight-archetype classification (Global Leaders, Regional Leaders, Cultural Capitals, Sustainable Cities, Industrial Hubs, Legacy Cities, Developing Megacities, Emerging Standouts) that runs parallel to the main ranking. The point is not to rank archetypes against each other, but to surface peer cities that share a structural narrative across continents. Buenos Aires and Ho Chi Minh City as Regional Leaders. Edinburgh and Reykjavik as Cultural Capitals. It is a clean idea, and worth borrowing.

So why am I writing this rather than nodding along? Because three editorial choices in the OEGCI methodology produce results that I do not think hold up to scrutiny, and the differences between their top 25 and ours are most useful when you understand the why.

Where we agree

The top of both indices looks similar where it should. New York at one and London at two on both. Paris at three on both. Tokyo, Boston, Sydney, Toronto, Seoul, San Francisco, and Singapore land within a handful of positions of each other across the two rankings. The major American and European Continental Cities (Washington, Chicago, Munich, Madrid, Milan) are within ten positions of where I rank them. Where the indices align, they align because the underlying signal is overwhelming. New York is where it is because it is the largest economy, the largest financial centre, and the largest cultural producer in a country that is also the largest economy, the largest financial centre, and the largest cultural producer. London is where it is because it has done what New York has done, in a country a quarter of the size, for longer.

The agreement at the top matters because it sets the credibility floor. Any methodology that doesn't put New York and London in the top three is doing something strange. From there, every divergence is informative.

Where we disagree, and the three methodological choices that explain why

One. The Governance category is measured at the national level

In the OEGCI, every city in the United States receives the same Governance rank: 161. New York, San Jose, Seattle, Boston, Atlanta, Phoenix, all 161. The same is true for every country: every Australian city scores 15, every Swiss city scores 10, every Chinese city scores 640, every Indian city scores 383.

The category is defined at the national level by design. The OE methodology document is upfront about it: "national governments, not just those at the city level, have a significant influence on these outcomes." That is true. But the consequence is that Governance, which is 10% of the composite, becomes a country-level penalty applied uniformly to every city in that country.

For high-governance countries this barely matters. For Chinese, Russian, Indian, Turkish, and Brazilian cities it is brutal. Beijing scores 4 on Economics and 6 on Human Capital, but 640 on Governance, which sinks its overall ranking to a place that no honest reading of Beijing's role in the world economy would support. Shanghai sits at 1 on Economics and 16 on Human Capital, but 640 on Governance, and ends up outside the top 50.

Our approach is different. The GaWC class adjustment that feeds our composite already encodes the institutional environment a global city operates in, but it does so as a city-level score, not as a flat country penalty. Beijing and Shanghai land at our 7 and 9. That is closer to what those cities actually are.

Two. The 30/25/25/10/10 weights are a competitiveness lens, not a quality-of-place lens

The OEGCI weights look balanced until you read what they are weighting. Thirty per cent on Economics is the GDP scale of the metro and its growth forecast. Twenty-five per cent on Human Capital is the workforce, education, and demographic vector. Twenty-five per cent on Quality of Life is the resident-experience layer. Ten per cent each on Environment and Governance.

San Jose ends up at four globally. Of the top five, three are American secondary tech cities: San Jose, Seattle, and the entire Bay Area distributed across two entries. The reason is simple. GDP per person at San Jose is around $228,000, which is the highest in the world by a substantial margin, and OEGCI rewards GDP per person heavily through both Economics and Human Capital indirectly. If your composite is 55% economic, San Jose is going to pop. If your composite reads "places where high-paid technology workers live," it is going to look like the OE ranking.

I am not arguing that San Jose should not be in the top 20. I am arguing that a 5% city in a 22 million metro area with two world-class universities is not the fourth most important city on Earth, and the only way you arrive at that conclusion is by weighting GDP per person at 30% and not weighting cultural reach, infrastructure scale, or institutional density at all.

Our composite weights GDP at the level of a tier bonus (+3 above $500B, scaled down from there) precisely so the rest of the formula does the work. The result is that San Francisco-San Jose, treated as the integrated Bay Area corridor it actually is, lands at five, which is the right place for it.

Three. AI preparedness is measured at the country level

The OEGCI introduces a new "government AI preparedness" indicator this year, which is one of the more interesting forward-looking metrics in any city index. It scores government AI policy, the maturity of the tech sector, and IT infrastructure capacity. It then applies the score uniformly to every city in the country.

This is the same flaw as the Governance scoring, applied to a metric where the city-level variance is enormous. Boston and Houston are not the same AI city. Bengaluru and Lucknow are not the same AI city. Toronto and Calgary are not the same AI city. The country-level proxy hides exactly the variance that makes the indicator interesting.

The two lists overlap on twelve metros and diverge on the rest. The pattern of divergence is not random. OEGCI rewards Anglosphere secondary cities (Melbourne 6, Sydney 7, Brisbane 23) and Nordic capitals (Stockholm 14, Oslo 17, Copenhagen 18, Geneva 25, Zurich 16). It penalises the East Asian giants (Beijing, Shanghai, Hong Kong) and the second-tier global cities of the developing world (Istanbul, São Paulo, Moscow). My ranking does the opposite, and I think the opposite is closer to right.

Sydney is a wonderful city. It is not, in any reading I find defensible, more globally significant than Beijing. Geneva, ranked 25th by OEGCI, sits at 70 on the metro completeness composite. The difference is not arithmetic. It is that OEGCI is asking a different question.

What OEGCI is actually measuring, and why our project asks something else

When you take all three editorial choices together, what OEGCI is actually measuring is closer to "where would an OECD-country corporate strategy team prefer to place a regional headquarters." That is a useful question. It is the question Oxford Economics' clients pay them to answer. It is also a question that systematically rewards small, wealthy, governance-stable, English-speaking cities and penalises large, complex, governance-mixed cities that drive a third of global GDP growth.

Our project asks a different question. The metro completeness composite tries to score the breadth and depth of globally-recognised infrastructure and activity concentrated in one place. Beijing scores high not because it is a pleasant place to be posted, but because it has built the institutional, financial, transport, cultural, and educational density of a global capital. Whether you would want to live there is a separate question. It belongs in a Liveability index, not an influence index.

The two questions are both legitimate. Where I push back is on the implicit framing in the OEGCI press materials, which presents the index as a "holistic assessment" of city attractiveness without acknowledging that the weighting is doing most of the editorial work, and that the editorial work is leaning toward a specific reader's question.

What we are taking from OEGCI

Three things. First, the archetype concept. Adding a small set of narrative clusters (we are introducing Greying Power, Cosmopolitan Capital, and Emerging Standout, which echo OEGCI's Legacy Cities, Cultural Capitals, and Emerging Standouts but use our own criteria) gives readers a way to find peer cities across continents. Second, a few of their indicators that we don't yet have but should: foreign-born share, housing burden, air quality. None of these are hard to source, all of them open up new editorial angles. Third, the Functional Urban Area discipline. We already build something close to FUAs through our county and municipality cross-sheet aggregation, but a more formal alignment with OECD definitions is on the roadmap.

What we are not taking from OEGCI is the weighting, the country-level scores, or the eight-bucket framework as a wholesale replacement for our tier system. The framework is the editorial product, and the editorial product is where I disagree with their choices.

A closing note on why this comparison matters

The point of writing this is not that the OEGCI is wrong. It is that no city ranking is neutral, and the best thing any ranking can do is make its choices visible and let the reader argue with them. The OEGCI publishes a 100-page methodology and report. The fact that you can argue with their methodology is a feature, not a bug. Most of the rankings further down the credibility curve do not give you that.

If you read both indices side by side, what you actually get is a set of three different cities. The cities Oxford Economics thinks have the most upside for an OECD-country business expansion. The cities I think have the most globally-recognised infrastructure right now. And the city you pick by ignoring both indices and trusting your own eyes. The third one is the one that matters. Indices like ours and OE's exist to help you check that intuition against something more rigorous than your last vacation.
